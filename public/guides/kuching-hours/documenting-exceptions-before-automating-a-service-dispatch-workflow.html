A Worksheet for Documenting Exceptions Before Automating Dispatch

Published by Blackstone Intelligence.

A practical worksheet for Malaysian service business owners and project leads to capture dispatch exceptions, edge cases and manual workarounds before automating a service dispatch workflow.

Why Exception Documentation Comes Before Automation

Automation projects tend to start with the happy path: a job comes in, a technician is assigned, the customer is notified, the job closes. That path is easy to draw and easy to demo. The difficulty usually sits in everything that does not follow it, and those cases are rarely written down in one place.

Before you configure rules or connect systems, spend time documenting the exceptions your dispatch team already handles by hand. The goal is not to automate everything at once. The goal is to know which decisions need a human, which can follow a rule, and which need a fallback when the rule does not fit.

This worksheet is a working document, not a formal audit. Treat the timings below as author heuristics for planning, not as benchmarks. A small team might complete a first pass in a few focused sessions. A larger operation with several service lines may need more. The value comes from writing things down in the same format so they can be compared and prioritised.

One useful side effect: exceptions often reveal unclear ownership. If nobody can say who approves an overtime call-out or a rescheduled slot, that ambiguity will be copied into any automated workflow unless it is resolved first.

Map the Current Dispatch Path in Plain Language

Start by describing how a job actually moves today, in the words your team uses. Avoid system names and job titles where possible. Describe actions instead. For example: request received, job details checked, technician selected, customer told, job done, paperwork closed.

For each step, note three things: who does it, what information they need, and what happens when that information is missing. This is where exceptions begin to surface. A missing address, an unclear fault description, a customer who wants a specific time window, a technician who is already committed elsewhere.

Keep the map short. One page per service line is usually enough for a first pass. If two people describe the same step differently, record both versions and mark the difference. That disagreement is itself an exception worth documenting.

A simple table works well: step, owner, required information, common gaps, current workaround. The workaround column is the most valuable one, because it captures the informal rules your team already trusts.

Build an Exception Register With Consistent Fields

An exception register is a list of cases that do not follow the standard path. Consistency matters more than completeness at this stage. Use the same fields for every entry so the list can be sorted and reviewed.

Suggested fields: an identifier, a short description, the trigger that signals the exception, who currently decides, what information they use, the current workaround, how often it is estimated to occur, and the impact if it is handled wrongly. Add a column for the proposed handling method: rule, human approval, or manual fallback.

Be careful with frequency estimates. If you do not have records, label the figure as an estimate from team memory and say so. Do not present guesses as measured data. A rough band such as rare, occasional or frequent is often more honest than a precise percentage.

Keep the register in one place and give it an owner. A register that lives in several spreadsheets will drift within weeks.

Classify Each Exception by Decision Type

Not every exception needs the same treatment. A useful classification separates three kinds of decisions.

The first is a rule-based exception: a clear condition with a clear outcome, such as a job outside a defined service area being routed to a partner or declined. These are candidates for automation once the condition and outcome are agreed.

The second is an approval exception: someone with authority must decide, such as approving a premium rate, an urgent call-out, or a schedule change that affects other customers. Automation can prepare the information and route the request, but the decision stays with a person.

The third is a judgement exception: the situation is ambiguous and depends on context, such as a long-standing customer with an unusual request. These need a documented fallback and a named owner, not a rule.

Misclassifying an approval as a rule is a common source of trouble. If in doubt, classify it as an approval for the first iteration and revisit later.

Write the Fallback Before the Rule

For each exception you intend to automate, write the fallback first. The fallback answers a simple question: if the automated step cannot complete, what happens next, and who is responsible?

A good fallback entry names the trigger, the person or role who takes over, the information they receive, and the expected response window. The response window is a service decision, not a technical one, so agree it with the people who will carry it out.

Consider a hypothetical example. A service business in a Malaysian city receives a request for same-day repair. The rule assigns the nearest available technician. The fallback covers the case where no technician is available within the promised window: the request is flagged to a dispatcher, the customer is offered the next available slot, and the dispatcher records the reason. Nothing in that example depends on a particular software product. It is a description of a decision path.

Writing fallbacks first keeps automation honest. It also gives you a test case: if the fallback cannot be described clearly, the rule is probably not ready.

Review, Test and Keep the Register Alive

Before any workflow goes live, walk through the register with the people who handle the exceptions today. Ask them to point out entries that are wrong, missing or out of date. Their corrections are more valuable than a polished document.

Then test the proposed handling against a small set of past cases, including awkward ones. Record what happened, not just whether it worked. If a rule produced a reasonable outcome but the customer was not informed, that is a finding worth keeping.

Set a review rhythm. A monthly review of new exceptions and a quarterly review of the whole register is a reasonable starting pattern, but treat it as a suggestion and adjust to your volume. Assign one owner for the register and one backup so it does not stall when someone is away.

Finally, keep the register separate from the automation configuration. The register explains why a rule exists. The configuration only shows what the rule is. When someone asks why a particular case is handled manually, the register should answer without needing to open the workflow tool.

Where This Fits in a Malaysian Automation Project

Malaysian service businesses often run dispatch across phone calls, messaging apps and spreadsheets, with knowledge held by experienced staff. That makes exception documentation especially useful: it turns informal judgement into something a project team can review and a new workflow can respect.

If you are working with an AI agency on dispatch automation, this worksheet gives the engagement a shared reference. It lets the agency see which decisions are rule-based, which need approval and which must stay manual, rather than discovering them after go-live. It also gives you a way to check proposals against your own documented reality.
