Running a Reception Rehearsal Before a Second Counter Opens

Published by Eyonic CCTV.

A practical guide to rehearsing a second service counter before opening day: splitting the queue, testing internal keyphone transfers, and checking smart lock door access for staff.

Why Rehearse the Second Counter Before It Opens

Consider a fictional branch in a Sarawak town that has always run one general enquiry counter. The manager has decided to add a second counter for document collection, and the new desk is built, wired and staffed on paper. The risk is not the furniture. The risk is that two counters change how people queue, how calls reach the right desk and how staff move between the front area and the back room. A rehearsal is a low-stakes run-through of those three flows before real customers arrive. Treat it as a dry run, not a launch party. Invite the receptionist, the person who will staff the second counter, a supervisor and one colleague who can play a confused customer. Set a fixed window, for example a two-hour session on a quiet afternoon, and write down what you will test. Keep the scope narrow: queue splitting, internal calls and staff door access. Anything else you notice goes on a separate list for later. The goal is to find the awkward moments while the branch is still empty, so the first real morning with two counters does not become the training session.

Step One: Split the Queue and Mark the Split Clearly

The first rehearsal task is queue splitting. Decide which services belong at counter one and which at counter two, then walk the route a customer would take from the entrance. In the fictional branch, counter one handles general enquiries and counter two handles document collection. Mark the split with a sign at eye level and a floor marker a few steps before the counters, not directly at the desk where a customer has already committed. During the rehearsal, have your colleague walk in as a first-time visitor and follow only the signs. Watch where they hesitate. If they stop and look around, the split is not clear enough. Test the awkward case too: a customer who needs both services. Write a short script for the receptionist, such as asking what the visit is about and directing the person to the correct counter before a number is issued. Keep the wording short and consistent. Note how long it takes to redirect someone, because that time adds up on a busy morning. The output of this step is a one-page queue map and a sign placement you have actually walked, not one you drew at a desk.

Step Two: Test Internal Keyphone Transfers Between Counters

The second rehearsal task is the internal call path. Two counters usually mean more transfers: a customer at the wrong desk, a colleague who needs a supervisor, or a back-room staff member who must confirm a document. Use the rehearsal to call between the counters, the supervisor's desk and the back room, and time how long each transfer takes. Check the basics: does the receiving phone ring where you expect, does the caller know who picked up, and can the call be returned if it drops? Write a short transfer list and place it beside each phone. In the fictional branch, the receptionist should be able to reach counter two, the supervisor and the staff room without leaving the desk. If your branch uses a keyphone or PABX setup, the rehearsal is the right moment to confirm which extension belongs to which desk and whether the labels match. Do not assume the installer's labels match your staff plan; walk to each phone and check. Record any extension that rings in the wrong place and fix it before opening day. The output is a tested extension list and a simple rule for when a call should be transferred rather than handled at the counter.

Step Three: Check Smart Lock Door Access for Staff Movement

The third rehearsal task is staff movement through the door behind reception. With two counters, more staff pass through that door in a morning, and the door becomes a shared boundary between the public area and the back room. This is where smart lock door access deserves a deliberate test rather than a quick tap. Eyonic's own review notes that smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and that for business use it usually checks whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is more appropriate. Use the rehearsal to confirm that each staff member who needs the door can open it, that a person who should not have access cannot, and that the backup entry method works when the main one is unavailable. Walk through the door with your hands full, as a receptionist carrying a file would. Note whether the door closes and latches on its own. The output is a short access list by role and a note of any door adjustment needed before the second counter opens.

Test Case: A Customer Joins the Wrong Queue

Build one scripted test case into the rehearsal: a customer joins the wrong queue. In the fictional branch, your colleague walks to counter two with a general enquiry. Run it twice. First, let the counter two staff member notice and redirect the person politely, with a short explanation and a handover to counter one. Second, run it as if the counters are busy, so the redirect has to happen without leaving counter two unstaffed. Watch for three things: whether the customer is told clearly where to go, whether the first counter is told to expect them, and whether any paper or number already issued is cancelled cleanly. A clean correction looks like this: the staff member greets the customer, names the correct counter, confirms the first counter has space, and cancels the wrong queue number so it does not sit in the system. Write that sequence down and keep it at both desks. The point of the test is not to catch anyone out. It is to make the correction boring and repeatable, so a wrong-queue moment on a real morning costs seconds rather than a complaint.

What to Record and What to Fix Before Opening

Keep the rehearsal output short enough that people will read it. A single page with four parts works well: the queue map, the tested extension list, the access list by role, and the wrong-queue script. Add a column for owner and a target date, and mark each item as fixed, accepted or deferred. Deferred items should have a reason, such as waiting for a sign to be reprinted. Set a second short rehearsal, for example one week later, to retest only the items that changed. This is an author heuristic, not a researched standard; adjust the timing to your branch. If the branch already has CCTV security systems covering the front area, check during the rehearsal that the new counter and the queue area are visible on the live view and that the recording is running. Camera placement decided before the second counter existed may leave the new desk at the edge of frame. A quick look at the monitor during the rehearsal is cheaper than discovering the gap later.

Where the Wider Setup Fits

The rehearsal covers the front of house, but the second counter sits inside a wider setup. If the branch is reviewing its phones, a keyphone or PABX arrangement can be planned around the extension list you tested, so the desk plan and the phone plan match. If the door behind reception needs more than a standalone lock, an installer can review the door condition and user roles before choosing between a smart lock and an access-control panel.
